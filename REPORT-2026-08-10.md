# prompten.xyz — engagement report, 10 August 2026

Written for someone picking this up later who wasn't here. It assumes you know
the stack (Next.js 14 App Router, Supabase, Vercel) and nothing about what
happened.

**Read `SECURITY-NOTES.md` alongside this.** It carries the live security
posture; this file carries the history and the reasoning.

---

## 0. The one-paragraph version

A free prompt-engineering course with ~820 accounts was hardened over several
sessions. The largest items: a live PII exposure was closed, password recovery
was found to be redirecting users to a domain owned by someone else and was
fixed, 54 accounts with missing database rows were repaired, dependencies were
patched, and a dismissible terms notice was shipped to collect acceptance from
the 763 accounts that predate the published documents. Three things are
permanently unknowable and are stated as such in §3 — do not let anyone
summarise them into reassurances.

---

## 1. What changed, by area

### Security

**Certificates PII exposure (closed, earlier session).** `public.certificates`
carried a SELECT policy readable by `anon`, exposing 31 users' names and email
addresses. Migration `000` dropped the policy and revoked `anon` SELECT.
Migration `001` replaced it with `verify_certificate(text)` — `SECURITY DEFINER`
with a pinned `search_path`, returning six columns and no email.

**Certificate ID entropy.** The original scheme carried ~31 bits; a birthday
collision was demonstrated empirically at #55,728. Migration `004` rotated every
certificate onto a 128-bit ID, keeping `legacy_cert_id` so old links still
verify.

**Certificates are server-issued only.** `/api/certificates/issue` derives
identity from a verified bearer token, computes score and grade from the
`progress` table, and ignores the request body entirely. It does *not* make the
underlying quiz scores trustworthy — answer keys ship in the client bundle and
grading runs in the browser, so `quiz_scores` is self-reported by construction.
That is an accepted property of a free unproctored course and the certificate
copy says so.

**Password recovery redirect (§4).** The largest incident of this engagement.

**All sessions revoked** — migration `009`, 8,299 sessions across 801 users, as
remediation for the recovery incident.

### Certificates

Course name on credential surfaces standardised to **"Prompt Engineering"** —
certificate face, the certificate disclaimer, the LinkedIn
`CERTIFICATION_NAME` parameter, and the `/verify` page. Before this the same
certificate named itself two different ways four lines apart.

Verified *before* changing anything that **no course name is stored per
certificate**: `verify_certificate` returns six columns and none is a course
name; the issue route writes none; `normalizeCert()` maps a fixed field list so
even an unused column couldn't reach a render path. All 33 issued certificates
therefore update together rather than splitting old cohort from new.

### Content and routing

Lesson URLs are slug-based and frozen. `scripts/check-course-integrity.mjs`
runs on `prebuild` and fails the build if lesson positions move, because stored
progress keys are positional (`"3-2"`) — a reordered lesson silently rewrites
history for every user.

Module 01's three lessons are readable signed-out; modules 02–08 are gated. The
gate is one predicate, `canOpen`, used by both the UI and the URL handler.

Quizzes live at `/course/<module>/<lesson>/quiz`, nested under their lesson.

### SEO

Public lesson bodies are **server-rendered**. Before this work, every lesson
returned a 489-character shell of CSS keyframes to a crawler. Now: 3 public
lessons render 4,483–6,279 characters of authored prose in the server HTML; the
23 gated lessons render title, brand and "LOADING…" and nothing else — verified
by searching gated payloads for authored sentences, not by counting bytes.

`src/lib/seo.js` holds one `indexableUrls()` used by both the sitemap and every
route's `robots` metadata, so the two cannot describe different sets. Currently
7 URLs: `/`, three document pages, three public lessons.

**Naming was consolidated.** The site ran three names at once: "PromptMastery"
in metadata and headers, "Prompten" on certificates and in the legal documents,
and "Prompt Engineering — Zero to Mastery" as the page title — the last
colliding with an unrelated established brand. Consolidated onto **Prompten**
because it matches the domain and was already the name in the published privacy
policy, so consolidating towards it never edited a document users had agreed to.

Two names remain and mean different things: **Prompten** is the site and the
issuer; **"Prompt Engineering"** is the course, used only where something is
named as a credential. The rule is written into the `SITE_NAME` comment in
`lib/seo.js` so nobody "fixes" the apparent inconsistency later.

### Legal pages

`/privacy`, `/terms` and `/about` are written, server-rendered, indexed and
linked from the homepage footer. Drafted against the NDPA 2023 and the NDPC
GAID 2025: privacy notice reachable from the homepage, erasure not conditional
on accepting new terms, 18+ with no route to verifiable parental consent below
that.

Contact addresses are `privacy@prompten.xyz` and `hello@prompten.xyz`, forwarded
via ImprovMX. Delivery was **confirmed by sending real messages and seeing them
arrive**, not inferred from DNS records resolving.

`DOCS_PUBLISHED` in `src/lib/docs.js` governs three surfaces together — sitemap
inclusion, footer links, and the registration checkbox wording — because holding
only one of them would be worse than holding none.

### Dependencies

`next` 14.2.3 → **14.2.35**, `ws` → 8.21.3, `nanoid` → 3.3.18, plus four
dev-tree packages. Production audit went from 4 findings (3 high, 1 critical) to
**2 (2 high, 0 critical)**.

The exact pin was preserved deliberately. `npm i next@14.2.35` rewrites
`package.json` to `^14.2.35`; that was reverted to a bare `14.2.35` so a clean
checkout cannot drift onto a Next version nobody has run the SSR verification
against.

### Consent

`profiles.consented_at` records that a user confirmed 18+ and accepted the
documents. Written **server-side only** — by `/api/auth/register` at signup, or
by `/api/consent` for the older accounts. Both stamp the server clock; neither
reads a timestamp from the client, because a timestamp the subject supplies is
not evidence of anything.

A **dismissible notice** (`ConsentNotice.js`) is live for accounts with
`consented_at IS NULL`. Accept writes through `/api/consent`; **Dismiss writes
nothing to the database**, only a per-user `localStorage` key. `localStorage` is
never consulted to decide whether someone has accepted — that question has one
answer and it lives in the database.

The blocking version (`ConsentGate.js`) is kept but not shipped. The lawful
basis for running the course is *contract*, not consent: nobody grants
permission for a service they signed up for. What the prompt buys is *evidence*,
which is worth having but not worth a blocking screen on a free course.

`CONSENT_MODE` is one variable with three values (`'off' | 'notice' |
'blocking'`) and the two booleans are derived from it. Two booleans can both be
true; one variable cannot hold two values, so the broken state has nowhere to be
written down. Check 8 of the integrity script enforces it and was **proven to
fail four ways** before being trusted.

## 2. Migrations — status

| # | What | State |
|---|---|---|
| `000` | Emergency lockdown of `certificates` RLS | Applied |
| `001` | `verify_certificate()` SECURITY DEFINER RPC | Applied |
| `004` | 128-bit certificate IDs | Applied |
| `006` | Adds `profiles.consented_at` | **Applied — but its two REVOKE statements are no-ops** |
| `007` | `pin_consented_at()` trigger | Applied — **this is the actual protection** |
| `008` | Backfill 54 missing `profiles` rows | Applied 2026-08-10 |
| `009` | Revoke all sessions | Applied 2026-08-10 |

**`006` and `007` deserve a paragraph, because `006` looks like it does
something it does not.** `006` ends with
`revoke update (consented_at) on public.profiles from authenticated`. In
PostgreSQL a column-level REVOKE cannot subtract from a table-level grant, and
Supabase grants UPDATE on the whole table to `authenticated` — so it removed a
privilege never separately held and changed nothing. Worse, the verification
query originally written into `006` read `information_schema.column_privileges`,
which reports a column as updatable whether the privilege came from the column
or the table: it returned the same reassuring answer either way.

`007` is the real protection — a `BEFORE UPDATE` trigger that raises `42501`
when `consented_at` changes and `current_user` is not `service_role` or
`postgres`. Verified by **attempting the forgery twice** from a signed-in
browser session against production — once as PATCH, once as UPSERT, because
PostgREST composes those differently — both refused with
`403 · 42501 · "consented_at is not user-writable"`, while a legitimate `bio`
save returned 200. Do not cite `006` as the protection. The lines are kept in
the file because a migration should record what was run, not what was meant.

`008` result, verified after the fact rather than from the run's own output:

```
before:  auth.users 819   profiles 765   missing 54   consented 1
after:   auth.users 820   profiles 820   missing  0   consented 1
```

Backfilled rows carry `name = ''` — not a name derived from the email address,
because a guess does not belong on a certificate — and `created_at` copied from
`auth.users` rather than defaulting to `now()`, which would have dated all 54 to
the moment of the backfill.

## 3. What is knowably unknowable

Three questions cannot be answered. Not "we didn't get to them" — the evidence
required does not exist and will not come into existence. Anyone who later
reports a number for these has invented it.

**1. The certificate-exposure access window.** `public.certificates` was
readable by `anon` for an unknown period before it was closed. Log retention
covers 24 hours. Whether anyone read those 31 names and email addresses before
that window, and how many times, cannot be established. The exposure is closed;
its history is not recoverable.

**2. How many people clicked a broken password-reset link.**
`auth.audit_log_entries` is **empty — zero rows, including the day it was
checked**. Either retention purged it or the events never persisted there. 46
recovery emails were sent between 25 April and 10 August, each carrying a link
that redirected to a third party's domain. How many were clicked is unknown and
will stay unknown.

**3. The true historical number of password resets.**
`auth.users.recovery_sent_at` **is cleared when a recovery is consumed** —
verified directly by watching a value disappear on completing a reset, with the
project-wide count falling 46 → 45. So it is a *pending* flag, not a history.
The count of 45–46 means "outstanding, unconsumed requests"; the historical
total is higher by however many people completed a reset, and that figure is
unrecoverable. An earlier statement in this engagement that "45 accounts have
requested a password reset since April" was wrong, and this correction is why.

A fourth, smaller one: **no repaired account was observed working.** See §6.

## 4. The password-reset incident

**What was wrong.** Supabase Auth had Site URL `https://promptmastery.com` and a
redirect allow-list containing `https://promptmastery.com/**`. `prompten.xyz`
appeared in neither. The application code was correct throughout — it requested
`redirectTo: ${window.location.origin}/reset-password` — but that matched no
allow-list entry, so Supabase discarded it and fell back to Site URL.

**Why it mattered more than a broken link.** `promptmastery.com` serves a GoDaddy
sales page, meaning it is **registered to a third party**, not unregistered.
Supabase's recovery flow redirects with the session in the URL fragment
(`#access_token=…&refresh_token=…`). Fragments never reach a web server, so
reading one requires JavaScript on the destination page — and parking pages run
scripts. There is no evidence any token was captured and no way to obtain any.
The mechanism was live from 2026-04-25 to 2026-08-10.

**How it was fixed**, in an order that never broke recovery in a new way:

1. Added `https://www.prompten.xyz/**` and `https://prompten.xyz/**` — purely
   additive. This **alone** restored correct behaviour, which proved the
   allow-list was the control and Site URL was only ever the fallback.
2. Changed Site URL to `https://www.prompten.xyz`.
3. Removed `https://promptmastery.com/**`.
4. Removed four `*.vercel.app` wildcard entries — a smaller instance of the same
   defect: a shared domain nobody here controls, sitting in the list that gates
   authentication.

Final state: Site URL `https://www.prompten.xyz`; allow-list exactly two
entries, both `prompten.xyz`.

**Remediation.** All sessions revoked (`009`). This is **not instantaneous**:
deleting sessions destroys refresh tokens immediately, but already-issued access
tokens are self-contained JWTs valid until expiry — an hour by default. The
long-lived credential is the refresh token and that is what was destroyed.
Rotating the project JWT secret would have been instant but disproportionate: the
anon and service-role keys are signed with the same secret, so it would mean
updating Vercel env vars and briefly breaking the site.

**`009`'s first draft was scoped backwards and was rewritten before running.**
It filtered on `recovery_sent_at IS NOT NULL`, which — per §3 — selects people
who were sent a link and did *not* use it. The population at risk is the
opposite. It would have signed out 45 unexposed users, missed everyone exposed,
and printed moving counts and passing assertions throughout.

**A postscript on diagnosis.** Sign-in appeared broken on one desktop for about
an hour. It was Starlink's DNS failing to resolve `*.supabase.co`. The site was
never down, and a Supabase project restart was performed chasing a symptom that
wasn't ours. **A different-network check costs thirty seconds and rules this out
before anything else** — try the site on a phone with wifi off, first.

## 5. The recurring failure mode

Worth knowing because it happened repeatedly and will happen again: **things
that report success while doing nothing.**

- `/api/consent` returned `{ ok: true, consentedAt: null }` for the 54 accounts
  with no `profiles` row — `maybeSingle()` returns null without error, the
  UPDATE matches zero rows without error, and the not-updated branch read null
  back and called it a won race. Fixed: 409 with a real message, and all three
  `ok: true` paths now require a truthy timestamp.
- A `useEffect` dependency on `user?.id` — always `undefined`, because the user
  object has no `id` (the UUID is separate state). The effect never re-ran. It
  built green and read correctly.
- `006`'s column REVOKE, above — and its verification query, which could not
  distinguish success from failure.
- Integrity check 7's first version skipped any line containing `//`, and the
  real offender ended in an eslint-disable comment. The guard against a vacuous
  fix was itself vacuous.
- During final verification, the SQL editor re-ran a saved
  `update … set consented_at = null` immediately before the SELECT that was
  supposed to prove dismissal writes nothing. It would have passed while proving
  nothing, on the single test the notice design rests on.

The habit that catches these: **prove a check can fail before trusting that it
passed.** Check 8 was deliberately broken four ways and watched to fail each
time. The `42501` trigger was verified by attempting forgery, not by querying
privileges.

## 6. Open items

**Custom SMTP for auth emails.** Reset emails arrive as
`Supabase Auth <noreply@mail.app.supabase.io>`. The dashboard itself warns the
built-in service "is not meant to be used for production apps". The email
*template* is already customised ("Reset Prompten.xyz Password") — only custom
SMTP changes the sender. This is the one email that most resembles phishing and
it currently comes from a domain unrelated to the product. Resend or Postmark,
free tier covers the volume, DNS already under control at Vercel.

**Remaining `next` advisories — unreachable by coincidence, not by
construction.** ~22 advisories plus `postcss` remain, fixable only by
`next@16`. They are not live because this app has no `middleware.js`, no
`next/image`, no Server Actions, no `rewrites`, no custom server, no i18n, no
CSP nonces and no `beforeInteractive` scripts. **Nothing in the codebase
prevents any of those being added.** There is no lint rule, no build guard and
no test that would fail. The day someone adds middleware or an `<Image>`, a
specific advisory class goes live silently, because `npm audit` output will not
change. `SECURITY-NOTES.md` maps each absent feature to the advisories it would
activate. Adding one of those features means upgrading Next as part of that
task, not as a separate one.

**Migrations `006`/`007`** — see §2. `006`'s REVOKEs are no-ops applied to
production; `007`'s trigger is the protection. Do not delete `006`'s lines
thinking they do something.

**Both test accounts are spent for notice testing.** `bloomstar042@gmail.com`
and `bloomstar042+notice@gmail.com` now have non-NULL `consented_at`, so neither
will show the notice again in any browser. To re-test:

```sql
update profiles set consented_at = null
 where id = (select id from auth.users where email = '<address>');
```

The `007` trigger permits this for `postgres` while still refusing any
client-side write.

**No repaired account was observed signing in.** `008`'s effect is verified by
data (`profiles` = `auth.users`, 0 missing) but nobody watched one of the 54 use
the site afterwards. It needs their credentials, and it cannot be faithfully
simulated: the register route sets `user_metadata.name` on every account it
creates, while all 54 have NULL metadata — which is exactly why they never
self-healed. Reproducing the state requires deleting a profile row *and* nulling
the metadata name.

**Correcting an overstatement made during this engagement:** the 54 were
described as unable to obtain a certificate with no way to satisfy the name
requirement. That is too strong. `upsertProfile` uses `onConflict: 'id'` and
**inserts when the row is absent**, so a user who reached the profile page and
saved a name would have created their own row. They were degraded — email-prefix
display name, a consent write that silently did nothing, certificate blocked
until they happened to set a name — not sealed off. `008` removes the need to
discover that path.

**Deferred, non-urgent:** `course_events` table lockdown; duplicate
`profiles`/`progress` fetches on each page load; the QUIZZES chunk split
(21.8 kB); landing-page SSR; sitemap "Couldn't fetch" status in Search Console
(submitted, fetch not yet demonstrated).

**Out of scope throughout:** notifying users about the certificates exposure.
That decision sits with a Nigerian data protection practitioner. For the record,
Search Console holds no history for this domain in either Google account, so
whether the `/verify` pages were ever indexed cannot be determined.

## 7. What to fix next, ranked by risk

**1. Custom SMTP.** Highest risk that remains, and it is not a technical risk —
it is a trust one. Password reset is the single email where a user is most
primed to suspect phishing, and yours arrives from `mail.app.supabase.io` with a
link to a third domain. Users who hesitate don't recover their accounts; users
who don't hesitate are being trained to click through exactly the pattern that
should make them stop. Half a day's work.

**2. The three build guards.** No `middleware.js`, no `next/image` import, no
`'use server'` directive — each failing the build with a message pointing at
`SECURITY-NOTES.md` and naming the advisory class it re-activates. Ten lines in
a script that already runs on every deploy. This converts the single largest
piece of standing security reasoning from a document nobody reads into a failing
build. Prove each one can fail before trusting it.

**3. `next@16`.** Clears every remaining advisory including `postcss` and the
eslint chain. Two majors: Next 15 makes `cookies()`, `headers()` and route
`params` async. The server-rendering behaviour this site's SEO depends on must
be **re-verified afterwards, not assumed** — public lessons carrying full prose,
gated lessons carrying none. Its own session, with the full pass at the end.

**4. The 29 empty-name accounts** (83 blank names total, minus the 54
backfilled). They can act for themselves, but they will hit the certificate wall
at the end of the course, which is the worst possible moment to discover it. A
prompt or a one-off email clears it.

**5. The repaired-account check.** A constructed test: register a throwaway,
delete its `profiles` row *and* null its `user_metadata.name`, confirm the
degraded behaviour, run `008`, confirm the repair. Lower priority than it
sounds — the data is verified — but it is the one claim in this report resting
on inference rather than observation, and it should not stay that way forever.

**6. Performance.** Duplicate `profiles`/`progress` fetches per page load, and
the QUIZZES chunk split. Real but not risk-bearing.
